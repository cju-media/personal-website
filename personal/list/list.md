## 5-4-26
Welcome to the beautiful month of May.

I had some screen scoring thoughts recently. Every film for the entirety of the existence of film has been scored by the orchesta. Since it was possible to put audio to film the orchestra has been the medium in which music has been expressed on the screen. Part of the reason is that the orchestra was the instrument that provided the widest variety of timbres. It was an existing medium with people trained to perform in it. Finding performers is very simple. If a composer experimented with a different type of ensemble then they risk set backs, increased costs and time to get the score to the picture. Economic and cultural pressures pushed composers to choose the orchestra.

That has broken in the modern times. Now that more and more composers start in DAWs than on staff paper the medium in which film composers choose to write in has changed. The DAW has been the real medium of film composition for over 2 decades now. Especially as sample libraries and physical modeling has improved so that low cost DAW production becomes sutiable for low budget productions, the DAW has become the instrument for media scores in the modern age. 

Since less and less composers start classically trained and work more and more with the language of the DAW and modern music production, the historic weight of the orchestra begins to lighten. You do not even need the orchestra to produced a finished score for a film. You can do it all in the DAW. Why do composers need to work within the restraints of the orchestra when the DAW gives you more flexibility with no downsides? A DAW produced score can sound just as good as a live orchestra score. 

I would argue that traditional orchestra scores sound **dated.** Thinking of the most notable film scores of the past half decade I gravitate towards the scores that have existed outside the traditional orchestra arrangement. Oppenheimer comes to mind. And that score was noticed for being different. It stood out from the crowd BECAUSE it is different. Another example is the [Zelda Tears of the Kingdom Trailer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uHGShqcAHlQ). Although there is an orchestra the composer uses a sax to carry the melody, very cool reverse vocal lines and non-western string instuments. The music stands out because it is not generic. Challengers is a another notable example. All 80s synths. I think we live in a time where getting access to an orchestra will become more and more out of reach for more and more projects. All to make your score sound generic. You can have a far more interesting sounding score without the cost and time to invest in a full orchestra recording.

A few years ago there was a quite public competition to rescore a scene from the Westworld show. There was a huge uproar about the winner. So many participant complained that they hated the winner. Some even shared their own submission as proof that the competition was rigged in some way? Let me say every submission I saw was a John Williams rip-off mickey-moused to hell and back. These young film composers that spent their lives listening to their film score professors profess that John Williams-esque scores were the end-all-be-all of media music. They all sounded exactly the same, were bland and uninteresting. The winning score? Something new and interesting. People can notice it. Composers who refuse to change with the times are left in the dust.

I think the future is in weird scores. Weird timbrally, not harmonically. Do you want to stand out? Score a cue with a sax quartet.

## 3-15-26
I saw Akhnaten yesterday. There was a good bit of I did not remember from when I saw this production a decade ago. Maybe I am a more attentive listener now. Maybe my tastes have mature. Maybe sitting in the balcony gave me a new perspective on the music (a lot of tuba, which I enjoyed). 

I generally like the slowmo evolution of action in this production in particular. However, contrast is the name of the game. *Everything* is so slow and sustained. The only real constrast is in the juggling and in the retrospective scene when the movements are not sustained. The retrospective scene itself sticks out. It is weird to have the framing device at the very end of the work. I understand from a theatrical perspective having the opening of the work be the birth/ascension of the title character but then why have the framing device in the final act? Maybe to speak of the lasting effects of Akhnaten? But I do not think that was well established in that scene. The professor was just giving instructions how to get the ruins of Akhanaten's temple. They have a bit of multimedia, flashing slides of the journey to Aten's city built ny Akhnaten. The opening movement with the herioglyphics of his name slowly fading on the scrim. I wish there was more of that. The closest you get to a big effects moment is the giant sun, representing Ak's new religion. The motif of the staircase reaching toward the sky was a good through line. 

Overall I wish there was more of a statement. Was Ak right or wrong to start a new religion? Is the message hubris? Was he too rash in the creation of this new religion? Is he an out-of-touch king? Was he trying to centralize his power? Did he genuinely believe in this religion and an uneducated populace revolted against his changes? I feel like there was room for those sorts of statements. If there was it did not quite cut through. 

Where *Einstein* was more about the vibes and the experience as a work of art. A painting in motion. Saytagraha and Ak have a more substantial story so the audience demands more from the overall statement of these works. At least in my opinion.

I realize I am falling behind on my daily commitments. Reading, writing, music, projects. It is a alot and I have no idea how to keep up. I wrote SOMETHING for The Way yesterday. I am not proud of it, I did not "finish" it. I guess the goal is not to have something completed it is just work on the practice of it. I just have to grind through until it feels comfortable. Even if I do not have a scene to compose for I have to do something. I can write words without a theme or a greater purpose, I should be able to do the same with my music. I should be able to write notes on a page without a greater theme and purpose. This is the importance of process.

All the greatest composers developed content using processes. How could Bach write a cantata every week? Process. The fugue is a very strict format, serialism is a very strict format. These formats gives the composer a way to generate content easily. I have long been of the opinion that early atonal work in the mid-19th century did not take off because composers in that style had to every single note as an option. Its like writing a sentence or a word with no gramatical rules or spelling rules. Having every option possible is limiting in a way. As I write this sentence I am generating words based on gramatical rules. Words and conjugations are given to me based on the context of the sentence it exists in. Of course, I can write completely random characters. Thats easy on a keyboard, but if I were writing this out, I would have to think consciously about the letters I would have to write. Just thinking of simple sentences to write would be a more fruitful endeavor in the effort of generating content.

This is why Scheonberg was successful in standardization of atonal music. He developed a way to generate content atonally. A grammar for that idiom which did not exist before formally.
